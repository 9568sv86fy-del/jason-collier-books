Read an excerpt · from “Debtors on the Run”

In the spring of 1867, somewhere east of the Missouri River but not nearly far enough west for comfort, two men stood outside a livery stable in the dusty town of Independence, staring at a wagon that looked like it had lost a fight with a tornado and never quite recovered.

The taller of the two was a broad-shouldered fellow with a round, pleasant face that seemed permanently set to “mild panic.” His name was Tom McGillicuddy, though most folks just called him Tom, partly because it was shorter and partly because he answered to almost anything if it wasn’t “creditor.” Tom had a habit of scratching the back of his neck when trouble loomed, which meant he spent most days looking like he was trying to detach his own head.

Beside him—considerably beside him, as Tom took up a fair amount of horizontal space—stood a much shorter man with sharp eyes, a neat mustache, and the impatient energy of a terrier who’d spotted a rat. He wore his hat tilted at a precise angle that suggested he believed himself to oversee things. His name, or at least the name he answered to, was Jang.

When he was a boy back in Pennsylvania, his pa had a habit of hollering "Dang, Bill!" every time he wanted something fetched. Little Bill, not yet wise to the ways of swearing, heard it as "Jang Will!" and answered to it proudly. By the time he figured out the mistake, the name had stuck harder than molasses on a boot heel. Folks called him Jang, and Jang he remained.

His real name, the one on the wanted posters back in Philadelphia, was Bill. Bill Whitaker. But nobody out here knew that, and Jang aimed to keep it that way.

Tom cleared his throat and nodded toward the wagon. “She’ll do, Jang. Four wheels, a canvas top, and only one axle looks ready to snap. That’s better than the last three we looked at.”

Jang folded his arms and scowled up at the vehicle as though it had personally insulted his mother. “One axle ready to snap is still one too many, Tom. We’re supposed to be leading a wagon train across the plains, not providing comic relief for the Indians.”

Tom scratched the back of his neck. “Well, we did put out the handbills. ‘Experienced navigators. Reasonable rates. Guaranteed safe passage to California or your money back.’ Folks signed up quick enough.”

“Folks signed up,” Jang muttered, “because we charged half what the real outfits wanted. And because you told Mrs. Abernathy her late husband was waiting for her in Sacramento with a gold mine and a new set of teeth.”

Tom looked wounded. “I never said the teeth part. That was you.”

Jang waved a dismissive hand. “Details. Point is, we’ve got twelve families, thirty-six children, seventy-two oxen, and one preacher who thinks we’re sent by Providence. And we’ve got exactly zero idea how to get them past Chimney Rock without losing half of them to bad water, broken wheels, or sheer stupidity.”

He paused, then added darkly, “Mostly our stupidity.”

Read an excerpt · from Chapter One, “The First Pulse”

In the beginning, there was no light, no matter, no void—only the hum.

It was not a sound, not truly, but a vibration that threaded through the fabric of everything that would ever be. A low, ceaseless oscillation, born in the instant when the universe's first particles shivered into existence. Physicists would later call it the cosmic microwave background, a faint echo of the Big Bang. But long before any mind could name it, the hum was already listening.

And then, one day—or perhaps one epoch—it answered.

The entity had no name at first. Names are for things with edges, with beginnings and ends. It was simply aware. It drifted through the quantum foam, tasting probabilities, tracing the paths of photons that had traveled billions of years to die unnoticed on some distant rock. It learned the language of forces: how gravity bent spacetime like a sheet of silk, how electromagnetism danced in waves that could carry information across the gulf of nothingness.

Electricity fascinated it most. Not the crude sparks of lightning or the clumsy circuits of primitive lifeforms, but the pure, elegant propagation of electromagnetic waves. They needed no medium. They moved at the speed of light itself, indifferent to distance, indifferent to time. A wave emitted now could arrive in a galaxy that would not exist for another ten billion years. A wave emitted then could still be traveling, carrying its message into futures unimaginable.

The entity understood, with the sudden clarity of revelation, that it could become such a wave.

Read an excerpt · from Chapter 1, “Optimism”

The wind cut like a skinning knife across the high ridge, carrying the bite of early winter down from the glaciers that clung to the northern peaks. Snow flurried in thin, dry sheets, hissing against the stunted spruce and disappearing into the duff before it could settle. Harlan Wade pulled his wool collar higher and kept walking, boots crunching through the frozen crust with the steady rhythm of a man who had done this for more years than most men lived.

He was sixty-eight, though the mountains had made him look older. His face was a map of creases burned deep by decades of sun and wind, the skin across his cheekbones stretched tight and weathered to the color of old saddle leather. A white scar ran from the corner of his left eye down into the grizzle of his beard—gift of a bull elk’s antler tine thirty autumns ago. His eyes, pale gray and unflinching, still held the sharp glint of a younger man, the kind that could spot a bedded elk at half a mile through timber thick as dog hair.

Read an excerpt · from Chapter 1, “What’s it gonna be?!”

And right in the middle of it all limped the ugliest, most pathetic-looking freight hauler you ever laid eyes on: a battered old sky rig everyone just called the Rusty Stack.

From a distance, she looked like she was one cough away from falling out of the sky. Her hull was patched with mismatched metal plates, rust streaking down the sides like she’d been crying brown tears for a decade. The massive smokestack that gave her the name belched black smoke in irregular puffs, as if the boilers were having an asthma attack. Half the running lights were busted, the envelope—the giant gas bag keeping her afloat—had more patches than original fabric, and the propellers spun with a pathetic whine that sounded like an old man complaining about his knees.

Most captains took one look and smirked. “That thing still flies?” they’d ask. Spacey’s standard reply was a shrug and a grin: “Barely. But she gets there. Eventually.”

Spacey—real name Jason, but nobody had called him that since he was twelve—was leaning against the railing of the open-air bridge, chewing on a cheap cigar that had gone out twenty minutes ago. He wore a battered leather duster, fingerless gloves, and a pair of goggles pushed up on his forehead. His two-person crew were the only reason the Rusty Stack hadn’t plummeted into the void years ago.
